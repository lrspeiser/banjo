# Puzzles on the machine (/play)

October 10, 2026. The machine page has a game: open `/play`. Each level is a small machine you cannot change, a goal, and a tray of pieces you add to make the goal happen. The engine runs the whole thing; nothing about the outcome is decided by the game.

## Playing a level

- **The goal** is a green box drawn in the scene ("the ball is in the cup") and a time limit. The engine checks it on every step: the goal happens when the named part's centre is inside the box.
- **The tray** shows a picture of each piece you may add and how many are left. You add one with its button at the top of the 3D view ("+ plank", with the same picture and how many are left; with none left it is greyed out, so a level that gives one plank takes one), so a piece is added, placed and set down without leaving the scene, and a see-through **ghost** of the piece appears: it is not in the machine yet. The pictures are drawn in the page from the piece's own parts, as the server would build it. A level whose goal is about a piece (level 4: the engine's piston) opens before that piece is placed, with its goal zone drawn, and says what it needs.
- **The ghost is placed at once**, where the middle of the view meets the scene, ready to change. Drag it, or click (tap) where it should go; on a computer, Follow the pointer makes it follow the mouse until a click.
- **Moving it, as 3D builders do.** A press anywhere within 40 pixels of the ghost takes hold of it, not only on its own few pixels (a mirror seen edge-on is three pixels wide), and the cursor turns to a hand there. It then slides on a level plane at the height it stands, by however far the pointer moves: it never jumps onto whatever is behind the pointer, so it can be pulled out from behind a wall. On a phone one finger anywhere in the scene moves it, and two fingers turn and zoom the view. A drag that does not start near it turns the view.
- **Guide lines** through the ghost, as 3D editors draw them: red along x and blue along z, out to the horizon, at the height it stands; the same, fainter, on the ground beneath when it stands above the ground; and a green line straight down to a ring on the ground under it. They show where it is against the rest of the level from any angle. Its controls are just below the scene, out of the way of the 3D view (on a phone right under the scene, with the page's header one short row so both fit on the screen; on a wider screen at the top of the side panel, kept in sight): chips pick a knob (length, turn, tip...), with - and + either side of its slider, and Set down. Where it stands is not a chip: the arrows and dragging move it. The full list of its knobs, x and z among them, is also in the panel.
- **The arrows** over the scene move it as you see it, 2 cm a press, and on while held: left, right, away from you and toward you go along whichever guide line (red x, blue z) is nearest that way on the screen, and each arrow is drawn in that line's colour, so turning the view can turn a blue arrow red. Up and down (green, like the vertical guide line) raise and lower whatever sets its height -- a panel's post, a cannon's bore, a knife's pivot, a whole ramp -- for a piece that has one; a plank or a mirror rests on what is under it and has none. The arrow keys do the same, a centimetre at a time (with Shift, ten), and Page Up and Page Down raise and lower it.
- **The hand and the eye.** In the middle of the arrows is a switch: ✋ while dragging moves the piece, 👁 while every drag and tap turns the view and leaves the piece where it is (the arrows still move it). Esc goes back and forth; ✕ throws the piece away. A big piece (level 2's knife) fills much of the view, and the eye is how to look round it. A new piece starts in the hand.
- **Energy against par, while you change it.** The ghost's bar says what the piece takes to make, with your other pieces, against the level's par: "It takes 34.1 MJ to make: 3.47 MJ over par (30.6 MJ)" for a 0.78 m plank on level 1, falling as it is cut shorter. The win card lists what each piece weighed and took, and on a win over par how much lighter the heaviest piece would need to be: "your plank 1 about 11% lighter". Level 1's par is its 0.70 m plank, which bridges the gap with its middle anywhere from x = 0.26 to 0.34 m; 0.66 m still does from 0.28 to 0.32 m (under par), and 0.62 m does not reach.
- **Turn it any way.** Every piece can be turned about the vertical (turn), tipped end up or down (tip), and rolled about its length (roll), by any angle: with the −15° and +15° buttons, with the sliders, or with keys (Q/E turn, R/F tip, Z/C roll; with Shift, one degree at a time). Shift and the mouse wheel turn it; Alt and the wheel tip it. Square it up puts it back level. Turning costs nothing. Everything the piece is made of turns with it: a cannon turned 20° fires 20° off to the side, and tipped up it fires upward. A piece tipped so far that part of it would go into the ground is lifted to stand on the ground.
- **Blue fits, red does not.** While you move, change and turn the ghost, the server works out where the piece would really be (a plank is lowered onto the highest thing under it) and whether it can go there. This takes a few milliseconds and runs nothing in the engine. A red ghost says why, for example "your mirror 1 goes 120 mm into wall; move it", and cannot be set down. Nothing of yours may go into anything solid, the level's or another of your pieces.
- **A cannon is fired by a pull cord** (level 3). Beside the level stands a trigger: an aluminium arm on a pin, with an iron weight hanging from one end on a cord with 2 cm of slack. As the run starts the weight drops, the cord pulls that end of the arm down onto an aluminium contact, and the weight keeps it there. The contact closes the firing circuit -- a switch closed while the two metal parts touch, measured from their shapes every step -- and the battery's current heats a coil on the cannon's powder until it burns (about 3 kJ, 0.4 s). Your cannon is wired to the trigger wherever you put it (the tray's `fired_by`); there is no timer. Level 5 closes its circuit the same way: an aluminium contact under the lever's left end, where the ball lands, and an aluminium plate under it. The ball comes off the ramp hard enough to knock the lever down and fly on -- a contact alone would touch for a moment, too short to heat the powder -- so the lever has a **ratchet**: past 4 degrees a pawl catches and it can go on down but not come back up, and the contact stays pressed on the plate while the coil lights the powder (the lever is a metre long so the ball lands on it rather than clipping its end).
- **Test a part: 🖐 Pull.** Beside Run it is a Pull switch. With it on, press on any loose part in the scene and drag: the engine pulls that point of the part that way, harder the further you drag (400 N a metre, at most 200 N), a line showing the string, and letting go lets go. It is a force on the part, not a hand in the world. Pulling starts the world if it was not running. The work your pulls do counts in the run's energy, and a run you pulled is **a test run**: it says whether the goal happened, earns no stars and is not kept -- to win, the machine has to do it. (On level 5 with no ramp, pulling the lever's end down closes the firing circuit and the cannon fires.) While Pull is on, clicking one of your pieces pulls it rather than picking it up.
- **A rope cut part way** (level 2) is weaker by what was cut: it holds the weight if what is left can carry it, and tears at the cut if not -- the piece above the cut staying on its arm, the piece below falling with the weight. A knife whose swing runs out part way through, or a blade that only clips the rope, leaves it hanging by what is left (docs/cutting-model.md, "A rope cut part way through").
- **A cord that sways** (level 6): the weight hangs on a chain of small oak segments that swings and is knocked about, with a cord through it carrying the weight exactly; the laser chars one segment away and the cord parts there, the chain with it, and the weight falls. Level 2's rope is still the stiff one, because a knife swung into a slack chain knocks it aside (docs/cutting-model.md, "A cord that bends and sways").
- **Nothing floats.** Everything in the world is held up by something: a fixed part stands on the ground, or is bolted to a fixed part that does, by a chain of them down to the ground. A piece that would hang in the air gets a stand, a concrete post from the top of whatever is under it (the ground, a table) up to its underside: a mirror stands on a post, a cannon's barrel on one, a ramp on two legs. A leg goes where it is clear, moved along the piece off whatever is in the way (level 5's lever), and a piece held by one leg needs no second; when no leg finds anywhere clear, the piece is red and says what its stand goes into. The stand is part of the piece: it shows with the ghost and in the tray's picture, and its concrete counts in the energy to make the piece. The levels' own parts keep to it too: level 6's laser sits on its post, and level 8's rope turns over two iron pulley blocks hung under the gantry's beam, the beam over the rope and the counterweight in line under it (it has no across knob). Loose things need no stand: gravity and contact hold them, or they fall. `scripts/machine_game.py` `hold_up` adds the stands as a level is built; tests/machine_game_tests.py checks every level, with its solution and with every knob at either end of its range.
- **Set it down** (or press Enter) and the engine builds it. From then on a loose piece is held only by gravity and contact: a plank too short for the gap falls in, and one too long for the ledges rests on the tables' tops a step up. Cancel (or Esc) throws the ghost away. **Pick up**, or clicking (tapping) the piece in the scene, turns a piece you have set down back into a ghost; Cancel puts it back as it was. During or after a run the level goes back to its start first, where pieces are placed. A ghost has to be set down or cancelled before you run the machine.
- **Where things will go.** Before a run, the scene shows a see-through path for every loose thing, and a see-through copy of the goal's thing where it ends up: the engine rehearsing the level as it stands, quickly and out of sight (with a ghost as if it were set down, so moving or turning a ghost shows where things would go). It is blue while a thing moves freely, green inside the goal zone, and after it hits something, that thing's colour, with a dot where it hit; a key over the scene says when the goal would happen and what each colour is. It is the engine's own answer, so the run follows it (level 1: the goal at 1.90 s in both). It changes a moment after anything is changed and stays up while the run plays. Each preview is a rehearsal of engine time: rationed per visitor (BANJO_PREVIEWS_PER_HOUR 300, BANJO_PREVIEWS_PER_DAY 6000) and two at a time.
- **Run it** is at the top of the scene, in one row with the pieces' add buttons (the arrows while a piece is placed) and Set up again, with the prediction's key at the end of the row. It builds the machine afresh and plays it at real time; while it plays it is Pause, then Continue, and after the run Run again. Set up again puts the level back at its start with your pieces. When the goal happens, a card just below the clock says **Level complete!** with the stars and the energy, and the world stops a moment later; the card sits at the top of the scene so what the machine did stays in sight below it (on a phone it is shorter: no piece-by-piece breakdown, its buttons in a row). A first win with a tool -- a plank, a knife, a cannon, a steam engine, a ramp, a mirror, a solar panel, a block and tackle -- says **Achievement Unlocked** instead, and which tool; each player's tools are kept with their progress (a player's wins from before tools were counted bring the tools of the levels won). After the last level it says so and offers level 1 again. When time runs out without the goal, the card says so and offers **Start over** (the level as it began, no pieces) or **Keep my pieces** (back to the start with them, to move or change): running the same pieces again would only do the same thing.
- **Energy: win on as little as you can.** A win is scored by the energy it took, two kinds, both real: making your pieces (each part's mass, from the engine's densities, times its material's embodied energy -- typical cradle-to-gate values from ICE, the Inventory of Carbon and Energy: oak 10.4 MJ/kg, iron 25, aluminium 155, glass 15, concrete 0.95; ice is the heat taken out to freeze it), and what came into the machine during the run, each once: what its batteries gave, as the engine measured it (a coil's heat, a laser's light, a motor's work all come out of a battery and are counted there, not again where they end up; the sun's is free), fuel burned in its heaters (level 4's firebox: its 12 kW for as long as the run has gone), and the powder in its charges at 2.8 MJ/kg. Making the pieces dwarfs the run (a 0.7 m oak plank is 30.6 MJ; a gram of powder 2.8 kJ), so smaller pieces and cheaper materials count most: on level 8 an iron counterweight, heavier, takes less energy to make than an aluminium one. Each level's par (`par_energy_j`) is its own solution's energy, measured, rounded up to three figures.
- **Stars**, from what the engine measured: one for the goal in time, a second for winning on no more than a quarter more energy than par, a third for par or less. If the chat placed the pieces, one star at most. The win card says what it took (pieces and run), against par and your best, and offers Next level, **Play again** (back to the start with your pieces, to win on less) and All levels.
- **The levels screen** (`/play` on its own, the header's Levels, or All levels): every level with its stars and your best win's energy against par, how many you have won, and which is next. **Players**: each name keeps its own progress -- stars, least energy, the last level played -- in this browser; add a player there and pick who is playing.
- **What the game page leaves out.** The sandbox (`/machine`) has the engine's own controls, and the game page hides them: the speed (a level plays at real time), Follow (the camera follows what moves), Path (the path is always shown), the list of levels (they have their own screen), and the engine's readings (stations, measurements, the list of what happened). The server's reliability check, the `trial` op (a machine run three times with every loose part nudged by up to 1.5 mm), is still there for scripts and tests, but has no button.

## The eight levels

| Level | What it teaches | The pieces | A solution, as tested |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Mind the gap | rolling | a plank | a plank cut to 0.7 m and set down at x = 0.3 drops into the ledges each table has at the gap, level with the tables: the ball reaches the cup at 1.9 s |
| 2. Cut it down | cutting | a knife pendulum | pivot at x = -0.08 m, 1.31 m up, a 0.6 m arm: the edge cuts the oak rope and the weight is in the bin at 0.7 s |
| 3. Knock it off | a powder charge, fired by a pull cord | a cannon | muzzle at x = 1.4 m, bore 0.48 m up, 1 g of powder: the cord pulls the trigger at 0.13 s, the cannon fires at 0.55 s, and the block is in the zone at 0.8-0.9 s |
| 4. Up she goes | steam | a steam engine | over the marker with a 12 kW firebox: the piston reaches the zone at 3.4 s |
| 5. Chain reaction | a chain | a ramp with a ball | the ball tips the lever, which closes the switch; the coil lights the cannon's powder; the ball knocks the block into the zone at 1.5 s |
| 6. Bounce the beam | light: reflection, and light heating what it lands on | two mirrors | a 5 kW laser's beam bounced around a wall by two mirrors at 45 and -45 degrees burns through the cord; the weight is in the bin at about 5 s (within 12 s) |
| 7. Catch the sun | sunlight: a panel facing the sun catches the most | a solar panel | a sun 15 degrees up behind the shed; the panel at x = 1.0, z = 0.4, turned -45 degrees and tipped 75 to face the sun squarely, takes 250 W of sunlight and stores 50 W, and the solar winch lifts the weight to the mark at 2.4 s |
| 8. Lift with less | mechanical advantage: a block and tackle | a counterweight on a block and tackle | a 4.7 kg counterweight through 3 pulleys, hung 1.75 m up, lifts the 13.6 kg crate to the shelf mark at 3.65 s for a cost of 11.4 |

How forgiving they are, measured: in level 2 the knife works with its pivot anywhere from 16 cm behind the rope's line to level with it, across about 25 cm of height. In level 3, 1 g of powder works from several places; 0.5 g falls short and 2 g throws the block past the zone. In level 7 the winch needs about 185 W of sunlight on the panel: lying flat in the sun it gets 65 W and the weight never moves; tipped only 30 degrees, or tipped without being turned toward the sun, is not enough; tipped 45 degrees toward the sun it lifts the weight by 3.2 s; in the shed's shadow it gets nothing, however it faces. The ghost of a panel says how squarely it faces the sun and how much sunlight that gives, if nothing shades it. In level 8, measured: 21.6 kg on one pulley lifts the crate at 0.67 s but costs 45; 13.6 kg on one pulley, the crate's own weight, does not move it at all; 7.4 kg on two lifts it at 1.9 s for 16.8; 4.7 kg on three, hung 1.75 m up, lifts it at 3.65 s for 11.4, but hung at 1.4 m it reaches the ground first and the crate stops 6 cm short; 4 kg on four would have to fall from higher than the gantry allows.

## Free build

The levels screen has a **Free build** card, and the header has a Free build link (`/play?level=free`). It is a table with no goal: every piece is in the tray, as many as you like, and you build whatever you want and press Run it. Nothing is scored. Your build is kept for the player who made it, in this browser, every time it changes, and it is there again when you come back. **Set out the example** puts a finished build on the table, and **Clear the table** takes everything off. The table's four corners, 2 m out from its middle each way, are marked by small concrete stones. The pieces are placed, moved, turned and set down the same way as on a level, and Run it, Set up again and Pull work as they do there.

The pieces, besides the levels' plank, ramp, cannon and steam engine:

- **Marble**: an iron, oak or rubber ball, 4 or 6 cm, set down on whatever is under it. On a slope it rests on the slope itself, not on top of the slope's box.
- **Track**: a board with a rail on each side, from its top end down by its drop over its length. It can be turned about the upright to point any way. Its far end is open (a marble rolls off it), a stop (the marble stays there), or a gate.
- **Shelf**: a short track tilted a little toward its gate, so marbles set down on it roll to the gate and wait there. This is how a marble stays up high until something lets it go.
- **The gate**: a flap hangs across the track from a bar on a pin over one rail, and the pin runs along the track. A marble pushes the flap along the pin, which the pin holds, so the flap doesn't move. Out past the rail is a short arm with a paddle on its end. Press the paddle down (drop a marble on it, tip a lever under it, or pull it) and the flap swings up out of the way. On its own it falls shut again. A **gate that stays open** has a catch that holds it once it is 20° up. Measured: three iron marbles wait at a closed gate. An iron marble dropped onto its paddle swings it up 56°: with the catch, all three roll off the end; without it, the gate falls shut before they arrive and they wait.
- **Flip-flop**: a rocker on a pin, tipped one way, with a fin standing up from its middle. Drop marbles on its middle from a track end above it, along the pin, and they go one way, then the other. The fin leans toward the low end, so a marble lands on the high side. Its weight tips the rocker over and it rolls off that end, leaving the rocker tipped the other way for the next marble. A board behind the rocker holds its pin and stops a marble that comes in fast. Measured: four iron marbles fed down a track went right, left, right, left. Two marbles that arrive together go the same way, as they would on a real one. An oak marble is too light to tip it.
- **Block**: a fixed post, wall, step or stop of any size, in concrete, oak or iron, set down on what is under it.
- **Dominoes**: a row of oak dominoes, 2 to 20 of them, standing on what is under the row.
- **Lever**: a seesaw on a pin on a block.

**The example**: a marble rolls down a short track onto a gate's paddle, and the gate opens and its catch holds it. The three marbles waiting on the shelf behind the gate roll off onto a track that runs down to a flip-flop, which sends them to the left and right. One track ends at a stop; the other runs out onto the floor into six dominoes, and they fall. Measured in the engine: the gate catches, marbles reach both sides and all six dominoes fall, in the run as written and in runs with every loose part nudged by up to 1.5 mm. The preview follows the marbles first. Its key names four of the things they hit, and how many more.

A thin turned part, such as a domino turned a quarter round, is built from the engine's grid of 2 cm cells. When its middle lies exactly between cells, no cell would be inside it, and the engine refuses a world with a body that has no matter. Such a part is now moved across its thickness by a quarter or half a cell and the machine's notes say so; none of the levels' parts needs it.

## Asking for help

The chat box on a level does two things:

- **Ask for a hint** sends your question, your pieces and what the engine measured in your last run (where things went, how fast, what they hit) to the model, which answers with a hint. It is never shown the level's solution.
- **Place it for me** asks the model for up to three ways to place the tray's pieces. The engine rehearses each and uses the first that reaches the goal. A knob just outside its range is brought to the end of the range, as the slider would. If none works, the engine searches from the one that came nearest: sixteen random settings of the same pieces first (nearness can mislead -- a ball flying past a lever comes near its middle from a ramp in quite the wrong place), then one knob at a time, keeping each change that brings the goal nearer and going on in that direction while it helps, up to 48 runs. "Nearer" counts the steps of a chain not yet reached (a level's `milestones`: in level 5 the ball hitting the lever, the switch closing, the cannon firing) and how near the first of them came. Measured with gpt-5-mini: levels 1, 2 and 4 placed by the model itself; levels 3 and 5 finished by the engine's search (level 5 from a ramp over the lever in 4 to 29 runs, three seeds).

## Adding a level

Levels are in `client/voxel-lab/levels.json`. Each has:

- `machine`: the fixed machine, a `banjo.machine.v1` declaration;
- `goal`: a title and a station rule, usually `in_zone` with a part, the box's centre and its size;
- `tray`: the pieces, each with `count`, `cost` (and `cost_per` a knob), and its knobs' ranges;
- `budget`, `par`, `time_s`, `hints` (shown to the player and to the model);
- `solution`, which is never sent to the page or the model.

The pieces (`plank`, `knife`, `cannon`, `steam`, `ramp`) are in `scripts/machine_game.py`. `tests/machine_game_tests.py` checks that every level's solution reaches its goal in three nudged runs out of three, that no level is solved with no pieces, and that a misplaced piece fails for a physical reason.

Level 6 burns the cord in seconds, as a real laser would: light heats the spot it lands on and chars the oak there away ([light-spots.md](light-spots.md)). Its own solution puts the weight in the bin at about 5 s, 3 runs in 3 (it took 20.3 s when a body's heat was spread through all of it).

## The site

The deployed site is the game and nothing else, open to anyone with no login: `scripts/voxel-lab.py --site game --public` (the Dockerfile's command) serves `/play`, the sandbox `/machine` it is built on, and what those pages load; `/` goes to `/play`; every other page and API answers 404. With no password in front of it, what costs money or a lot of engine time is rationed instead: the chat (which spends the model's key) to 12 requests per visitor an hour and 400 a day, and the reliability check to 30 per visitor an hour and 2,000 a day (BANJO_CHAT_PER_HOUR, BANJO_CHAT_PER_DAY, BANJO_TRIALS_PER_HOUR, BANJO_TRIALS_PER_DAY). The server runs at most BANJO_MACHINE_SESSIONS live levels at once (10 as deployed, from the Dockerfile; 4 by default). Nobody's game is ended while they play it: a new level takes the place of one whose page has closed or gone quiet for 15 s (a page polls every second, and tells the server when it is closed), else of one whose player has not built or started anything for 3 minutes; otherwise the newcomer is told the site is busy and to try again in a minute or two. A level closed while it sat idle is built again when its player presses Run it. A player can **tell us what happened** from the box under the verdict: their words, with the level and the pieces placed, are written to the server's log as a `FEEDBACK` line and to `feedback.jsonl` beside the machines' logs, rationed to 10 an hour per visitor and 500 a day; nothing that names the player is kept. A first visit opens **How to play** (five steps) above the levels; the header's link opens it again. `--public` is refused without `--site game`. The other labs stay in the code and run locally without these switches.

## Real time

A level plays at real time. When something breaks or dents, the engine solves the impact in its lattice of cells. Played live, that solve runs on the engine's worker while everything else carries on and the pair that met is held still, for at most 50 ms of world time. If the answer is not back by then, the machine waits for it, because a body held longer misses what it should have been doing. Rehearsals, tests and reliability runs always wait, so their answers are exactly the physics'. How long a solve takes is being worked on (see docs/machine-physics-roadmap.md).
